# Interview script — eliciting each slot by question

The question bank that fills the runtime's six contract slots from a writer's answers instead of a
corpus. Work the slots in order. Show the profile filling in live through the chosen surface. The
runtime contract (`voice-gate/references/voice-profile-contract.md`) is the schema authority; this
script only elicits what fills it.

## The discipline that runs under every question

Self-report drifts toward the voice the writer wishes they had. Three habits hold the line:

1. **Probe every abstraction.** When the writer says "punchy", "warm", "plain", "lyrical", do not
   record the adjective. Ask the follow-up that turns it into a behavior or a line: "Show me a
   sentence that sounds like you," "What would the un-you version say, and what do you change?"
2. **Prefer a line to a description.** The strongest answer to any question is the writer typing a
   real sentence in their voice. Ask for one wherever you can; it becomes the slot's evidence.
3. **Mark the basis.** Every answer the writer only asserts is `self-reported (unverified)`. An
   answer backed by a sample line (Step 3 grounding) becomes `sample-grounded`. The calibration gate
   tests the unverified ones first.
4. **Handle claims about uncovered forms.** When an answer references a form outside the covered set
   (a guardrail "in my essays" implying an email register that was never scoped), do not silently
   apply it to a covered form. Either add that form to the scope and ask its register questions, or
   record the claim and mark it `out-of-covered-scope`. The covered forms are what the profile
   speaks for.

Spread the interview across the forms the profile covers (essay / newsletter / email); several
slots are form-dependent.

## Slot 1 — Identity and register map

Lead from the floor, not the flashiest register.

- "When you are writing with no audience to impress — a note to yourself, a plain paragraph — how do
  you sound? Type one line."
- "Name the forms you want this profile to cover: essay, newsletter, email, something else."
- For each form: "How does that plain voice change here? Looser, tighter, warmer, more guarded?
  Give me an opening line you would actually write in this form."
- Dosing probe (sets up Step 4): "When something is personal, how much of it reaches the page in an
  essay versus a work email?"

Record the plain register first, then the per-form shifts, each with the line the writer gave.

## Slot 2 — Hard guardrails (mechanical, non-negotiable)

These are the most testable answers in the interview, so press for absolutes.

- "Any punctuation you never use, or always use? Em dashes, semicolons, the Oxford comma, exclamation
  points?"
- "Contractions: do you use them everywhere, never in composed work, only in casual writing?"
- "Words or constructions you refuse — openings you would never write, sign-offs you always use, a
  phrase that is not you?"
- Probe for the real rule, not the aspiration: "Is that a rule you actually hold, or one you think
  you should? When did you last break it?" A rule the writer cannot keep is not a guardrail; mark it
  `self-reported (unverified)` so grounding and the gate can check it.

Record each guardrail as a testable rule. A guardrail the writer asserts but cannot demonstrate is
flagged for the gate.

## Slot 3 — Figurative signature

The single most aspirational slot. Writers overstate their imagery.

- "Do you reach for figures — metaphor, simile, images — or do you mostly write plain and concrete?"
- "If you use figures: one dominant image per piece, or many small ones? Give an example you are
  proud of."
- "Which figurative moves feel not-you? Overwrought similes? Abstractions standing in for a thing?"
- Hard probe: "Type the most figurative sentence you have written recently." Then judge it against
  the claim. If the writer claims rich figuration and produces none, or claims plain and produces a
  stack of metaphor, record the gap; grounding or the gate resolves it. "Near-zero figuration, plain
  register" is a valid, common, honest answer.

## Slot 4 — Native pressures

Present the nine and have the writer choose. Evidence is the work, not biography.

- "Here are nine pressures a piece can run on: danger, desire, secret, shame, contradiction,
  decision, dread, curiosity, longing. Which two or three does your strongest writing actually run
  on?"
- "Which are off-voice for you — ones you reach for but they fall flat? Any you overuse as a crutch?"
- Ground it in a piece, not a feeling: "Think of something you wrote that worked. What was pulling
  under it?" Record the pressure and the piece, not a psychological story. Do not build a dossier;
  name the pressures and stop. This is the only interior the gate consumes.

## Slot 5 — Preserve list / signature moves

What reads as the writer, so the gate's preserve governor does not sand it.

- "What do editors or readers try to 'fix' that is actually you on purpose? Fragments, a dry aside, a
  short sentence after a long one, repetition, a rule you break deliberately?"
- "Give me a move you would fight to keep."
- Silence-rule probe (sets up Step 4): "When something is hard, do you name the feeling or do you let
  it sit under the details? Type how you would handle it."

Record each move with the line the writer gave. The more specific, the better the gate protects the
voice.

## Slot 6 — Known surface tells (optional)

The writer's own tics that flatten them when overused.

- "When your writing is bad, what does it do? Your specific tell — over-explaining, a favorite word,
  a tidy ending you reach for, a rhythm you fall into?"
- "Anyone ever call out a habit in your writing? What was it?"

Record any habitual move that flattens this writer specifically. The universal surface check runs
without this slot; it is optional, never fabricated to look complete.

## Closing the interview

Before drafting, read back the filled slots and let the writer correct them. Then apply Step 4
(dosing and silence), draft from the profile template (Step 5), and produce the calibration gate
(Step 6). The interview ends where the gate begins; the gate, not the interview, is what proves the
profile.
